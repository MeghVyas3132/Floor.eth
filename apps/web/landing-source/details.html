Definition

What the rating is, before what it is for.

Floor is a security rating for onchain collateral. It reads the live control surface of an asset — every route that can create or release it — and publishes a grade a lender can act on, with the evidence to recompute it.

Pinned
Every grade names the block it was computed at, so two people reading it are reading the same chain.
Lender-defined
Thresholds come from the lender’s own risk framework. Floor encodes the rules; it does not invent them.
Bounded
A response can only reduce new exposure. Floor never moves funds, and never restores exposure on its own.

Why Floor exists

The failure it was built after, and why that failure was invisible to everything watching.

Collateral can look safe while its weakest route stays exposed.

The risk is in the effective setup

A bridged token depends on more than its contract: bridge routes, verifiers, owners, delegates, upgrade keys, timelocks and rate limits all shape who can create or release it. A field left at zero inherits a default somebody else controls, and an optional threshold can make “three verifiers” mean “any one of three”.

None of that is hidden. It is spread across contracts on several chains, in fields whose meaning depends on which other fields are set — which is why it goes unread until it matters.

Most monitoring waits for a change

Guardians and alerting fire on movement: a transaction, a parameter edit, a price break. That catches drift and misses everything that was already wrong and simply stayed that way.

Floor grades standing state instead. The question is not “what changed since yesterday” but “what is true at this block”, which is the only question that has an answer when the dangerous setting has not moved in a year.

One route, one verifier, twelve and a half months.

rsETH · Ethereum mainnet

  1. 2 Apr 2025

    A wallet acting as the adapter’s delegate sets one route to a single required verifier, sending the change directly rather than through the owning multisig.

  2. Aug 2025 → Apr 2026

    Control moves to a 3-of-6 Safe. The verifier setting is never touched again, so nothing that watches for change has anything to report. At the end of it, 24 of 26 Ethereum routes stood on one verifier.

  3. 18 Apr 2026

    That verifier’s own nodes are fed a false view of the source chain and outside providers are knocked offline. A forged message releases 116,500 rsETH.

  4. +22 blocks

    The first borrow against the released collateral lands about four and a half minutes after the drain. The first lender freeze comes roughly 88 minutes after it.

Why this is live now

There is now a standard to fail
Aave’s DAO approved a risk framework by Snapshot vote in July 2026. Collateral is expected to meet it continuously, not once at listing.
Defaults moved, settings did not
After the incident, LayerZero said protocol defaults would be no less than a three-of-three verifier configuration. A default is not an application’s effective setting, and most applications set their own.
One fewer independent reader
Chaos Labs stepped away from Aave in April 2026, leaving a single risk provider on the largest lending market.
A second stack to read
CCIP 2.0 launched in September 2026 with its own verifier sets and thresholds, so the same posture questions now have to be asked twice per asset.

When the weakness sits in something many markets share rather than in one asset, the only instrument most lenders have is a broad freeze — on 29 October 2026 Aave froze 292 reserves while one exploiter’s position was assessed. Knowing which markets actually depend on a given verifier, library or key is what makes a narrower answer possible.

How it works

One pass, left to right: what gets read, what decides, and what the decision is allowed to do.

From onchain state to a bounded response.

Floor reads the live configuration of the bridges, keys and libraries a collateral asset depends on, grades that reading against a lender’s published rule pack, and publishes the reads the grade was computed from. Enforcement is the last stage and the narrowest one: an opt-in adapter can lower a cap or stop new borrowing. It cannot move anyone’s funds.

Resolve the graph
Every bridge route an asset can arrive on, with the verifier set, receive library, control keys, timelock, oracle and mint authority behind it — read at one block so the result can be recomputed later.
Apply the rule pack
Defaults, optional thresholds and shared operators are resolved to the setting actually in force, then checked against a versioned lender policy and the issuer’s declared Manifest.
Publish the reads
A grade ships with the block, the onchain reads and the rule version it came from, so a third party can reproduce it from public data instead of trusting the label.
Bound the exposure
Where a lender has opted in, a falling grade can lower a cap or close new borrowing. Nothing is liquidated, nothing is moved, and turning it back on is the lender’s decision.

For issuers: Floor Check and Manifest (planned)

A Manifest states the security settings an issuer intends to run — which verifiers are required, which library is pinned, what delay a change has to sit through. Floor Check simulates a proposed Safe transaction against that statement before anyone signs it, and the same check runs in CI so drift is caught as a failing build rather than as an incident.

For risk teams: attestations and feeds

Grades are signed and can be mirrored to other chains, with the history kept alongside the evidence. Lenders, curators, insurers and exchanges read the same posture data, which is the difference between four teams maintaining four private spreadsheets and four teams disagreeing about one public number.

Current build, live on testnets: LayerZero effective-state resolver, Aave rule pack v0, a live read of all 24 Aave V3 markets, fork replay and browser verifier. A Chainlink CRE workflow writes the grade on Ethereum Sepolia, CCIP mirrors it to Base Sepolia, and there it cut a Morpho vault’s cap to 0. Floor Check, the Manifest and the Safe guard are planned. Rating CCIP-bridged assets is on the roadmap. Nothing runs on mainnet.

What we check

The rules a grade is computed against, the four grades, one measurement we ran ourselves, and the limit of every place Floor can act.

A security grade with reasons you can inspect.

Thresholds belong to the lender, not to Floor. The first rule pack encodes the Aave Risk Framework approved by Snapshot vote, including its minimum of three independent verifiers on every Aave-exposed route — and it is versioned, so a grade always names the edition of the rules that produced it.

Verifier threshold
At least 3 independent verifiers per route, counted as required, never as required plus optional
Control and upgrades
Bridge and library changes gated by a timelock, with delegates and Safe thresholds resolved to the path actually usable today
Route limits
Inbound and outbound bounds checked separately, because only the inbound one bounds what a forged message can release
Libraries and dependencies
Pinned receive library, oracle, mint authority and the implementation address behind every proxy

OK

Meets the selected lender rules at the scanned block.

DEGRADED

Weaker than required, with headroom left; a lender may lower caps.

BREACHED

A hard rule fails; new exposure can be reduced where a lender has opted in.

UNKNOWN

Evidence missing or a dependency unmapped. Not a pass, and not a zero.

One of those rules, measured rather than asserted.

Aave’s framework asks that a bridge bound how much a single inbound message can release. To find out whether that bound exists, Floor calls each adapter at a pinned block from the LayerZero endpoint address, with a correctly formed credit from the route’s own configured peer, and classifies the result by revert reason. Simulation only, on a fork — never a broadcast.

Of 64 LayerZero adapters over Aave-listed tokens on 8 chains (rerun 2026-11-07): 31 had no inbound bound: they accepted a credit for their entire escrow, or minted the maximum amount. 4 enforced a real per-message limit, 4 returned a test error we count as neither a pass nor a failure, 4 held nothing to bound, 15 had no active peers, and 6 are LayerZero v1, which this probe does not cover. The probe refuses to run at all unless the same call is first rejected from a non-endpoint address and from a wrong peer.

This measures the application layer, not drainability: a real attack still has to defeat the verifier layer first. Floor does not claim to have reproduced the community’s multi-chain “35 of 39” figure — the lists overlap and agree on every adapter both could probe, and the rest is the next step.

Where Floor can act, and where it cannot.

CI and pre-sign check (planned)
Catches a planned config, library or delegate change that lowers the floor — before anyone signs it.
Blind to anything sent outside the pipeline, such as a transaction from an EOA delegate.
Safe guard, fail-open (planned)
Catches weakening through the Safe’s own signed path, including batched calls and configs that only look like three verifiers.
Blind to modules, EOA and timelock delegates, bridge default changes, other chains — and to a quorum that removes the guard first. Not deployed on funded Safes.
Continuous monitoring
Catches all of the above, plus default changes, owner and delegate changes, modules and the same asset on other chains. This is the source of truth.
Has nothing to block with. It can only publish and feed the enforcers.
Lender enforcers
Catches new exposure taken against collateral that is already BREACHED.
Leaves existing positions alone. That is a design decision, not a gap: no forced unwinds.

Enforcers only ever reduce new exposure. Floor never moves funds, never pauses a protocol, never blocks repayment, withdrawal or liquidation, and never re-enables collateral by itself. A lender or curator has to opt in, and a human decides when exposure is restored.
